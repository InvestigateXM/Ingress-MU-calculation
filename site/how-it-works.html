How cells and densities decide the Mind Units

The Method page defines how the Mind Units (MU) of a field are calculated. This page shows its main ideas on five fixed example fields. Every number is computed by the calculator of this project and stored with the page; nothing here is calculated in the browser or requested from the API. The coordinates are example coordinates, not necessarily portals.

Each animation is a series of steps. Use Play, Previous and Next, or the slider. When the drawing has keyboard focus, the left and right arrow keys change the step, Home and End jump to the first and last step, and the space bar starts and stops. Under each animation the numbers of the example are listed.

The first animation shows a small excerpt of the population data (13 pixel values, the pixels read by the sample points of the two cells shown). Everywhere else only values derived from it are shown: densities of cells, overlap areas and contributions. All example fields are smaller than 10,000 km², in the size range for which the method is well established. Results for very large fields (from a few hundred thousand km²) are likely but not confirmed to be correct in every case.

1. From pixels to the density of a cell

The population data is a grid of 30 arc-second pixels. The smallest cells that the covering uses, cells of level 13, do not follow this grid. The density of such a cell is the mean of 16 values read from the grid, one per sample point. A cell of level 12 does the same with 64 values.

2. A field inside one smallest cell

This field has a covering of one level-13 cell. Its contribution is the area of the field times the density of the whole cell; the regional minimum and final rounding then give its MU.

3. A field that contains a combined cell

A candidate whose four children are terminal becomes terminal itself; normalisation also merges four final siblings. A fully contained cell is already terminal and needs no split. Its density is the mean of its children's densities, but their contributions can differ slightly because their areas are unequal. At the field edge, unequal overlaps can make the difference much larger.

4. The budget of 20 cells

For a candidate at level 6 or finer with more than one intersecting child, splitting requires result + queue + children to fit within 20. A single intersecting child is always followed, and cells coarser than level 6 must split. Other candidates stay whole when the budget would be exceeded. Their density is the mean over the whole cell.

5. A smaller field with more MU

The covering depends on the field. A field G that lies completely inside a field F can earn more MU than F, because the two coverings average different cells.
